Water Heater Repair in Austin, TX

Tank and tankless heater diagnosis, repair, and leak checks across Austin.

Call (737) 263-1705 Ask a question

A cold shower and a wet patch outside a heater closet both get called a broken water heater, but they lead to different work. From the hallway, a heater that has stopped heating shows up as cold or quickly fading water at the shower, while a failed tank or valve often leaves water at the closet threshold, drain pan, or garage floor. We handle both heating faults and water leaks on tank and tankless units in Austin, Rollingwood, West Lake Hills, and Lost Creek.

We start by separating a fixture-side complaint from a heater-side fault, then trace the problem to the tank, burner, element, thermostat, valve, or supply line. A heater producing only cold water without a leak can sometimes wait for a planned repair, but water at the base, repeated discharge from the relief valve, or a gas odor should not wait. A leaking tank can damage flooring and framing, while a combustion fault or pressure problem needs attention before the heater runs again.

No hot water or rumbling

Customers often call because the shower turns cold halfway through, the water stays lukewarm at every tap, or the heater makes a popping or rumbling sound after a hot-water draw. Sediment on the bottom of a tank can make those sounds, while a failed heating element, thermostat, burner, or ignition component can leave the water cold. A tankless unit may also shut down during a draw and show an error code on its control panel.

Water around the tank base, a wet drain pan, rusty water from the hot side, or water running from the temperature-and-pressure relief valve point us toward a different set of parts. A relief valve can open because of pressure, temperature, or an expansion-tank problem; a seam leak points toward the tank itself. These complaints can sound alike over the phone, but the failed part, labor, and replacement materials can differ substantially once we inspect the heater.

Heater inspection

We begin the callout by reading the rating plate, checking the installation area, and looking for water at the shutoff, unions, drain valve, relief valve, and tank seam. We measure supply pressure, confirm the thermostat setting, and check for a normal hot-water temperature range of roughly 120 to 140 degrees Fahrenheit. On electric units, we isolate power before testing voltage, heating elements, thermostats, and wiring; on gas units, we inspect the burner, ignition, vent connection, and flame behavior.

A focused diagnosis commonly takes about 20 to 40 minutes, depending on access to the panel, burner compartment, and piping. After the test, we can often replace a compatible element, thermostat, igniter, thermocouple, relief valve, or drain valve during the same callout when the part matches the heater. We schedule follow-up work when a tank needs replacement, a model-specific component must be ordered, access requires additional preparation, or permit and sign-off steps apply.

Tank age, access and shutoff valves

Tank age affects the repair path because a corroded jacket, rusted seam, or deteriorated anode connection can make a single-part repair less practical than it first appears. A heater in an open garage gives us a different route to the controls and piping than one behind a closet door, above a ceiling access hatch, or in an attic. Stored boxes, narrow stairs, finished walls, and a drain pan with limited clearance can add work before we reach the failed component.

A seized water shutoff, corroded union, or brittle supply connector can also change the scope because we may need to renew fittings that will not disconnect safely. Model-specific control boards, tankless heat exchangers, and burner assemblies may require an ordered part rather than an on-site replacement. Replacement work can also involve venting, drain-pan connections, electrical disconnects, gas piping, or required sign-off, depending on the heater and its location.

Model number and heater access

Before calling, read the model and serial number from the rating plate on the tank jacket or from the cabinet panel of a tankless unit. Tell us whether the heater sits in a garage, closet, attic, or utility room, and whether a door, stairs, or stored boxes restrict the route. If you know the installation age or have a photo of the burner, control, or leak point, keep it with the unit.

Note whether hot water has stopped at every tap or only after a short run, and whether water is actively leaving the drain pan or tank base.
