Toilet Repair in Austin, TX

Repairs for running toilets, weak flushes, leaks, and faulty fill valves.

Call (737) 263-1705 Ask a question

Most people start by holding the flush handle down, jiggling it, or waiting for the tank to finish filling. Those are sensible first checks because a loose chain or hung-up flapper can sometimes settle back into place. The trouble returns when a worn flapper, sticking fill valve, or bent handle arm has already started to fail.

A plunger also makes sense when the bowl drains slowly or the water rises after a flush. It can clear paper near the trapway, but it will not correct a weak flush caused by a low tank water level, blocked rim holes, or a damaged flush valve. We check the bowl, tank, supply line, and base rather than treating every flush problem as the same blockage.

A toilet that only needs an occasional handle adjustment can usually wait a short time if the bowl and floor stay dry. A tank that keeps refilling, a bowl that rises toward the rim, or water at the base should not wait because water can run across flooring or pass beneath the toilet seal. We cover toilet repairs across Austin, Rollingwood, West Lake Hills, and Lost Creek.

Toilet keeps running

People often call us because the toilet keeps running after the flush, even though nobody has used the bathroom. That sound can come from a flapper that leaks into the bowl, a fill valve that does not shut off, or water reaching the overflow tube. A tank may also make a whistle, hiss, or repeated short refill as the valve struggles to close.

Other calls start with a weak flush, a handle that moves without flushing, or a bowl that fills too high before draining. Water around the toilet base can point to a failed wax ring, loose closet bolts, condensation, or a crack in the porcelain. We inspect the tank parts and the toilet connection because a running tank, poor flush, and wet floor can look alike from outside, while each fault uses different parts and carries different repair work.

Tank and bowl inspection

We begin the callout by flushing the toilet and watching the tank refill, bowl level, handle arm, chain, flapper, and overflow tube. We compare the water level with the molded mark inside the tank and check whether water continues into the bowl after the fill valve should stop. A dye test can show flapper seepage into the bowl within about 15 minutes.

We close the supply valve, inspect the braided connector and shutoff, then check the tank bolts, mounting bolts, and toilet base for corrosion or movement. We also test the flush handle and measure whether the chain has enough slack to let the flapper seal. A basic tank-part repair commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes after we identify the failed component.

We can often replace a worn flapper, fill valve, handle, chain, or supply connector during the same visit when the matching part is available. A cracked bowl, seized mounting hardware, damaged flange, or toilet that must be removed for drain access may need separate scheduled work. If the toilet connects to an in-wall carrier or a concealed valve, we explain the access needed before opening the wall or tile.

Fill valve, shutoff, and access

The age and design of the tank parts affect the repair work. A common flapper or universal fill valve may fit the tank directly, while an older flush valve, proprietary seal, or unusual tank lid can require a matching part from the manufacturer. We identify the part before replacing it so the flush volume and water level work with that toilet.

Access around the toilet also changes the work involved. A clear bathroom floor gives us room to remove the tank lid, isolate the supply, and test the base, while a toilet boxed in by cabinetry, tile, or a tight wall can make the shutoff and mounting bolts harder to reach. Rusted tank bolts or a corroded supply connector can add work because those parts may break during removal.

A shutoff valve that has seized can require work beyond the tank repair, especially if it will not fully stop water to the toilet. A part that must be ordered can also move the repair into a return visit. In a condominium or shared building, access to a water riser or a building manager’s sign-off can affect the scope before we disturb a valve or wall opening.

Tank stamp and bathroom location

Before calling, check which bathroom has the faulty toilet and whether the tank is running, the bowl is rising, or water is appearing at the base. If you can safely remove the tank lid, look for the make or model marking inside the tank or on the porcelain near the water line. A clear photo of the flush handle, fill valve, and flapper can help us identify the likely replacement part.

Tell us whether the toilet sits beside a cabinet, behind a pedestal sink, or in a room with limited access to the shutoff valve. If you know when the toilet was installed or when the last tank part was changed, that can help distinguish an aging seal from a newer part that has slipped out of adjustment.
